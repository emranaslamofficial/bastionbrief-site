If you have read [how hackers target Instagram accounts](/blog/how-hackers-target-instagram-accounts/), you know that most takeovers rely on stolen passwords, forwarded codes and fake login pages. This tutorial turns that knowledge into settings. Each step is short, and the order matters: we start with the foundations an attacker would go after first.

:::note Before you start
Instagram updates its app regularly, and menu names occasionally change. At the time of writing, most security settings live in **Accounts Center**, which you reach from your profile by tapping the menu icon (☰) and then **Accounts Center**. If something has moved, the search bar in Settings will usually find it.
:::

## Step 1: Check your contact details

Your email address and phone number are how Instagram verifies you during recovery. If either is out of date — or belongs to an old account you no longer control — you could be locked out, or someone else could use it to reset your password.

Go to **Accounts Center → Personal details → Contact info**. Confirm that every email and phone number listed is current and is yours. Remove anything you do not recognise or no longer use.

:::warning If you see an email or number you did not add
That may mean someone has already had access. Remove it, then continue with the rest of these steps immediately — particularly the password change and two-factor authentication.
:::

## Step 2: Secure the email account behind Instagram

This step happens outside Instagram, but skipping it undermines everything else. Whoever controls your email can reset your Instagram password.

In your email provider's settings, make sure the email account has:

- a password used nowhere else;
- two-factor authentication turned on;
- recovery options (backup email or phone) that are current.

Our guide on [two-factor authentication](/blog/two-factor-authentication-explained/) covers how to choose the best method for your email.

## Step 3: Set a unique, strong password

Go to **Accounts Center → Password and security → Change password**.

Choose something long and unique. A passphrase of four or more unrelated words is easy to type and very hard to guess, especially with some personal twist. The key word is *unique*: if your Instagram password has ever been used on another site, change it now. [How to create strong passwords](/blog/how-to-create-strong-passwords/) explains why length beats complexity, and a [password manager](/blog/password-managers-explained/) can generate and remember one for you.

## Step 4: Turn on two-factor authentication

This is the single most important step. Go to **Accounts Center → Password and security → Two-factor authentication**, choose your Instagram account, and select a method.

| Method | How it works | Strength |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Authentication app | An app on your phone generates a new six-digit code every 30 seconds | Strong — codes are created on your device and cannot be redirected by a SIM swap |
| Text message (SMS) | Instagram texts you a code when you log in | Good — much better than nothing, but vulnerable to SIM swapping |
| WhatsApp | Codes are delivered through WhatsApp, where available | Good — depends on the security of your WhatsApp account |

If you can, choose **authentication app**. Instagram will guide you through linking a free app such as Google Authenticator, Microsoft Authenticator or the built-in authenticator in many password managers.

### Save your backup codes

After enabling two-factor authentication, look for **Additional methods → Backup codes**. These single-use codes let you log in if you lose your phone. Write them down or print them, and keep them somewhere safe and offline — not in a screenshot on the same phone.

:::danger Never share a login code
Two-factor authentication only protects you if the codes stay with you. Instagram will never ask you to send a code to anyone, and neither will a genuine friend. If someone asks, it is a scam.
:::

## Step 5: Review where you are logged in

Go to **Accounts Center → Password and security → Where you're logged in**. You will see a list of devices and approximate locations.

If you see anything you do not recognise — an unfamiliar device, a location you have never been to — select it and log out. Approximate locations can be imprecise, especially on mobile data, so focus on devices you genuinely do not recognise. If in doubt, log out of everything except the phone in your hand, and sign back in elsewhere afterwards.

## Step 6: Turn on login alerts

Go to **Accounts Center → Password and security → Login alerts** and make sure alerts are enabled for your Instagram account. You will be notified when someone logs in from a device Instagram does not recognise, giving you a chance to react quickly.

## Step 7: Remove connected apps you do not use

Over the years, many of us grant access to quizzes, scheduling tools, analytics dashboards and "follower tracker" apps. Each one is a door.

In Instagram's settings, look for **Website permissions → Apps and websites**. Remove anything you do not recognise or no longer use. Be particularly wary of any service that once asked for your Instagram password directly — legitimate integrations use Instagram's official login screen instead.

## Step 8: Learn to verify official Instagram emails

Phishing emails pretending to be from Instagram are common. Instagram lets you check whether an email genuinely came from it: under **Password and security**, look for the option that shows **recent emails** sent by Instagram. If a security email you received is not listed there, treat it as fake.

Take a moment now to find this screen, so you know exactly where it is when you need it.

## Step 9: Tighten who can message and tag you

Many scams arrive by DM. Reducing who can reach you reduces the number of scams you see.

- **Message controls:** decide whether message requests from people you do not follow go to a separate requests folder, or are not delivered at all.
- **Hidden words:** filter messages and comments containing words commonly used in scams.
- **Tags and mentions:** limit who can tag or mention you, to reduce impersonation and spam.
- **Private account:** if you do not need a public audience, a private account limits who can see your posts and followers — which also limits what a scammer can learn about you.

These settings are mostly in **Settings → How others can interact with you** and **Settings → Who can see your content**.

## Step 10: Prepare your recovery plan

The time to plan for recovery is before you need it.

:::checklist Your recovery kit
- [ ] Backup codes are stored offline
- [ ] I know the official recovery page: instagram.com/hacked
- [ ] My email account is secured with its own two-factor authentication
- [ ] I have a recent copy of my data (Accounts Center → Your information and permissions → Download your information)
- [ ] I know that paid "account recovery experts" contacting me online are scammers
:::

If you ever need it, our guide on [what to do if your online account has been hacked](/blog/what-to-do-if-account-hacked/) takes you through the recovery process step by step.

## Keeping it secure

Security is not a one-off task. Every few months, spend five minutes repeating steps 5 and 7 — checking where you are logged in and which apps are connected. And keep the two most important rules in mind every day: do not log in through links you were sent, and never share a code.

:::takeaways
- Secure the email account behind Instagram first — it is the master key.
- Use a unique password and two-factor authentication with an authentication app.
- Save backup codes offline, and never share login codes with anyone.
- Review active sessions and connected apps regularly.
- Use Instagram's list of recent official emails to spot phishing.
:::

:::faq
Q: Is a private Instagram account more secure?
A: A private account does not change how hard it is to log in to your account, but it limits who can see your posts, followers and personal details. That reduces what scammers can use to target you or your friends, so it is a useful privacy layer.
Q: What happens if I lose the phone with my authentication app?
A: Use one of the backup codes you saved to log in, then set up two-factor authentication again on your new device. This is why saving backup codes offline is so important.
Q: Should I use the same authentication app for all my accounts?
A: Yes, that is fine and convenient. Just make sure the app itself is protected by your phone's lock screen, and that you understand how to back up or transfer it when you change phones.
Q: How often should I change my Instagram password?
A: There is no need to change a strong, unique password on a schedule. Change it immediately if you suspect it has been exposed — for example, after a phishing attempt or a breach of another site where you reused it.
:::
