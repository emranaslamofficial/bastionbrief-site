Security mistakes are rarely about ignorance. Most people know, roughly, that they should use better passwords and be careful with links. The mistakes persist because the secure option feels inconvenient, the risk feels distant, or the habit formed years ago and never got revisited.

This is a list of twelve common mistakes, why they happen, and what to do instead. You do not need to fix all of them today. Pick the two or three that apply to you and start there.

## 1. Reusing the same password

**Why it happens:** Remembering dozens of unique passwords is impossible without help, so people pick one good password and use it everywhere.

**Why it matters:** When any one of those sites is breached, criminals try the same email-and-password combination on many other services automatically. One leak becomes many compromised accounts.

**The fix:** Use a unique password for every account, which in practice means using a [password manager](/blog/password-managers-explained/). Start with your email, banking and social media accounts.

## 2. Skipping two-factor authentication

**Why it happens:** It sounds technical, and people assume a strong password is enough.

**Why it matters:** Two-factor authentication stops most account takeovers that start with a stolen or guessed password.

**The fix:** Turn it on for your email first, then everything else important. An authenticator app is better than text messages, and passkeys are better still. Our [two-factor authentication guide](/blog/two-factor-authentication-explained/) explains the options.

## 3. Postponing updates

**Why it happens:** Update prompts always appear at inconvenient moments, and "remind me tomorrow" is easy to tap.

**Why it matters:** Many updates fix security weaknesses that are already publicly known — which means attackers know about them too.

**The fix:** Turn on automatic updates for your operating system, browser and apps. Let devices update overnight.

## 4. Trusting messages because they look official

**Why it happens:** Logos, formatting and professional language signal legitimacy, and genuine companies really do send security alerts.

**Why it matters:** Copying the appearance of a real message is trivial. Phishing relies on exactly this assumption.

**The fix:** Judge messages by where they really come from and where their links really go, not by how they look. Better still, never act on links in unexpected messages — go to the service directly. See [how phishing attacks work](/blog/how-phishing-attacks-work/).

## 5. Sharing verification codes

**Why it happens:** The request comes from someone who seems trustworthy — a friend, a "support agent", a delivery driver — and sharing a short code seems harmless.

**Why it matters:** A verification code is often the final key to your account. Sharing it can hand over control immediately.

**The fix:** Adopt an absolute rule: codes are never shared, with anyone, for any reason. This single rule defeats many of the tactics described in [how hackers target Instagram accounts](/blog/how-hackers-target-instagram-accounts/).

> If someone asks for a code that was sent to you, the answer is no. There is no exception.

## 6. Leaving the email account weakly protected

**Why it happens:** People think of email as just another account.

**Why it matters:** Your email can reset the passwords of almost every other account you own. Whoever controls it controls a great deal.

**The fix:** Give your email the strongest protection you use anywhere: a long, unique passphrase and the best two-factor method available.

## 7. Oversharing on social media

**Why it happens:** Social platforms are designed to encourage sharing, and individual details seem trivial.

**Why it matters:** Birthdays, pets' names, schools, employers and travel plans help attackers guess security answers, craft convincing phishing and impersonate you.

**The fix:** Audit your public profiles and limit what strangers can see. Our guide to [improving your online privacy](/blog/improve-online-privacy/) walks through it.

## 8. Installing software from anywhere

**Why it happens:** Free versions of paid software, cracked games and handy tools from unknown websites are tempting.

**Why it matters:** These are among the most common delivery methods for malware, especially infostealers that harvest saved passwords and logged-in sessions.

**The fix:** Install software only from official app stores and developers' own websites. If something paid is offered for free, assume there is a hidden cost. See [malware explained](/blog/malware-explained/).

## 9. Having no backups

**Why it happens:** Backups feel like a chore until the day they are needed.

**Why it matters:** Ransomware, theft, accidental deletion and hardware failure can all destroy files permanently.

**The fix:** Turn on automatic cloud backup for your phone, and back up your computer regularly — keeping at least one copy that is not permanently connected to it.

## 10. Ignoring security alerts

**Why it happens:** Alert fatigue. After dozens of harmless notifications, people stop reading them.

**Why it matters:** "New sign-in from an unrecognised device" and "Your password was changed" alerts are often the first and best chance to stop an intrusion.

**The fix:** Treat genuine security alerts from your accounts as urgent. Check them by opening the app or website directly — not by tapping links in the alert.

## 11. Using public computers and networks carelessly

**Why it happens:** It is convenient to check email on a hotel computer or connect to any available Wi-Fi.

**Why it matters:** Shared computers may be infected or may keep you logged in. Fake hotspots and Wi-Fi sign-in pages can capture credentials.

**The fix:** Avoid logging in to important accounts on shared computers. On public Wi-Fi, use trusted networks, keep devices updated and never enter account passwords on a Wi-Fi sign-in page. See [how public Wi-Fi can put your data at risk](/blog/public-wifi-risks/).

## 12. Assuming "I'm not a target"

**Why it happens:** People imagine attackers carefully choosing important victims.

**Why it matters:** Most attacks are automated and opportunistic. Criminals target millions of people at once and take whoever is vulnerable. An ordinary account is valuable for scams, resale, spam or as a stepping stone to others.

**The fix:** Assume you will be targeted, because statistically you will be — not personally, but as part of a crowd. The basics on this list put you ahead of most of that crowd.

## A two-minute self-check

Not sure which of these mistakes apply to you? Each row below has a quick test you can do right now, and how long the fix usually takes.

| Mistake | Quick test | Typical fix time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Password reuse | Could you name a password you use on more than one site? | 10 minutes for the important accounts |
| No two-factor authentication | Does your email ask for anything besides a password on a new device? | 5 minutes per account |
| Postponed updates | Is there an update notification waiting on your phone or laptop right now? | 2 minutes to enable automatic updates |
| Weak email security | Is your email password unique and protected by 2FA? | 10 minutes |
| No backups | If your phone fell in a lake today, would your photos survive? | 5 minutes to turn on cloud backup |
| Oversharing | Can a stranger see your birthday or phone number on your profile? | 15 minutes |

If you answered the "wrong" way on two or more rows, start with email and two-factor authentication. Those two changes deliver more protection per minute than anything else on this page.

### Why small fixes beat big ones

It is tempting to wait until you have time for a complete security overhaul. In practice that day rarely comes. Attackers mostly exploit the easiest available gap, so closing the biggest gaps quickly — even imperfectly — protects you far more than a perfect plan you never start. Fix one thing today, another next week, and let the improvements compound.

## The three fixes that solve most of the list

If the list feels long, focus on three habits. Between them, they address most of the mistakes above:

:::flow Three habits, most of the protection
- A password manager: Unique passwords everywhere, plus a warning when you land on a fake site.
- Two-factor authentication: A stolen password is no longer enough to get in.
- Pause and go direct: Never act on links or codes from unexpected messages.
:::

Add automatic updates and a regular backup, and you have covered the overwhelming majority of everyday risk. Our [beginner's cybersecurity checklist](/blog/cybersecurity-checklist-for-beginners/) puts all of this into a step-by-step plan.

:::takeaways
- Most security incidents start with an ordinary, understandable habit.
- Password reuse, missing two-factor authentication and skipped updates are the biggest culprits.
- Never share verification codes, and protect your email above everything else.
- Assume you are a target — automated attacks do not discriminate.
- A password manager, two-factor authentication and a "go direct" habit fix most of the list.
:::

:::faq
Q: What is the most common cybersecurity mistake?
A: Password reuse is one of the most common and most consequential, because a single breach can expose many accounts. Not using two-factor authentication is a close second.
Q: I have made several of these mistakes. Am I already hacked?
A: Not necessarily. Many people have made these mistakes without consequences — yet. Fix the most important ones now: unique passwords for email and banking, two-factor authentication, and updates. Check your email address on a breach-notification service such as Have I Been Pwned.
Q: Is it really that dangerous to delay updates for a few days?
A: A few days is usually low risk for most people, but some updates fix vulnerabilities that are already being exploited. Automatic updates remove the decision and the risk of forgetting.
:::
