When a Facebook account is taken over, the instinct is to panic and try everything at once. It helps to think of recovery as a sequence. Some actions are urgent because every minute gives the intruder more time to lock you out further. Others matter just as much but can wait until the immediate danger is over.

This guide is organised by time. Start at the top and work down. If you are currently locked out, jump straight to the section for your situation in the first 15 minutes.

## First 15 minutes: regain control

### If you can still log in

You are in the best possible position. Act quickly and in this order:

1. **Change your password** from a device you trust. Choose one you have never used before.
2. **Log out of all other sessions.** In your security settings ("Where you're logged in"), choose the option to log out of all sessions.
3. **Check your contact details.** Remove any email address or phone number you did not add, and confirm yours are still there.
4. **Turn on two-factor authentication**, ideally with an authentication app. See our [two-factor authentication guide](/blog/two-factor-authentication-explained/) for help choosing a method.

### If you are locked out

Go to Meta's official recovery page at [facebook.com/hacked](https://www.facebook.com/hacked) and follow the prompts. Do this from a browser you trust, by typing the address yourself rather than following a link.

:::tip Check your email for a "this wasn't me" link
When someone changes the email address or password on a Facebook account, Meta usually sends a notification to the previous email address. These messages often include a link to reverse the change and secure the account. Look in your inbox (and spam folder) for recent security emails from Facebook, and use that option if it is there.
:::

During recovery, Meta may ask you to confirm your identity — for example, by entering a code sent to an old email or phone number, identifying friends, uploading a photo of an ID document or recording a short video selfie in some regions. Follow only the steps shown on Meta's own pages.

:::danger Beware of recovery scammers
After posting publicly that you have been hacked, you may be contacted by people offering to recover your account for a fee. They often claim to be "certified ethical hackers" or to have contacts at Meta. They are scammers. No one outside Meta can restore your account, and paying them will only cost you money.
:::

## First hour: limit the damage

Once you have control — or while you wait on the recovery process — protect the people and things connected to your account.

### Warn your friends

If the attacker has been sending messages from your account, friends may be receiving scam links or requests for codes or money. Post a short message (or ask a family member to post one) letting people know your account was compromised and that they should ignore any recent requests from it. Use another channel, such as a text message, for close contacts.

### Secure your email account

If your Facebook account was compromised, check the email account linked to it. Change its password if there is any chance it was exposed, and turn on two-factor authentication. Whoever controls your email can reset almost everything else.

### Check where else you used that password

If your Facebook password was reused anywhere else, change it on those sites now, starting with email, banking, shopping and other social media. This is also the moment to consider a [password manager](/blog/password-managers-explained/), which makes unique passwords effortless.

## First day: inspect everything the attacker could have touched

Now look for anything the intruder changed or created. Attackers sometimes leave behind ways to get back in.

:::checklist Account inspection checklist
- [ ] Recent posts, stories and comments — delete anything you did not create
- [ ] Messenger — check for messages sent in your name
- [ ] Contact info — only your own email addresses and phone numbers remain
- [ ] Two-factor authentication — enabled, using a method you control
- [ ] Apps and websites — remove anything you do not recognise
- [ ] Login alerts — turned on
- [ ] Name, profile picture and date of birth — unchanged
- [ ] Groups you administer — check for new admins or posts
:::

### If you manage pages, ad accounts or business assets

Business assets are a common target because they can be monetised. Check carefully:

- **People and admins:** in Meta's business tools, review everyone who has access to your pages, ad accounts and business portfolios. Remove anyone you do not recognise.
- **Ad accounts:** look for campaigns you did not create and for new or unfamiliar payment methods. Contact your card provider if you see unauthorised charges.
- **Connected Instagram accounts:** if your Facebook and Instagram accounts are linked through Accounts Center, check the Instagram side too. Our guide to [securing your Instagram account](/blog/secure-instagram-account/) covers the key settings.

## First week: harden and learn

The emergency is over, but this is the best time to make sure it does not happen again.

### Work out how it happened

Knowing the entry point tells you what to fix. Think back over the previous days and weeks:

| If you remember… | It was likely… | Read |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Entering your password on a page from a link | Phishing | [How to identify a fake login page](/blog/how-to-identify-fake-login-page/) |
| Sending a code to a "friend" | Social engineering | [Social engineering explained](/blog/social-engineering-explained/) |
| Using the same password on another site | Credential stuffing | [How to create strong passwords](/blog/how-to-create-strong-passwords/) |
| Installing an unusual extension, app or file | Possibly malware | [Malware explained](/blog/malware-explained/) |
| Nothing at all | Any of the above, or a compromised email account | [How Facebook accounts get compromised](/blog/how-facebook-accounts-get-compromised/) |

### Check your devices

If you suspect malware — for example, the account was taken over again even after you changed your password — scan your devices with reputable security software, update your operating system and browser, and review installed browser extensions. Consider changing important passwords again from a device you are confident is clean.

### Report impersonation

If the attacker created fake profiles using your name or photos, report them through Facebook's impersonation reporting option. Ask friends to report them too.

### Keep monitoring

For a few weeks, keep an eye on login alerts, unusual emails about your account, and bank or card statements if a payment method was attached.

## Helping someone else recover

Often the person whose account was taken over is a parent, grandparent or friend who asks you for help. A few points make that easier and safer:

- **Let them stay in control.** Sit with them while they go through Meta's recovery steps on their own device, rather than taking their password and doing it for them.
- **Do not reuse their old password** or create one you will both "remember". Set up a unique one, ideally saved in a password manager on their device.
- **Set up two-factor authentication with them**, and make sure they know where the backup codes are kept.
- **Explain what happened in plain terms.** Knowing that the "friend" asking for a code was actually the attacker helps them spot the next attempt.
- **Agree on a way to check with you.** A simple "Is this real?" text before clicking or sharing anything prevents a great deal of harm.

Patience matters. People who have just been hacked often feel embarrassed. Reassuring them that these scams catch careful, intelligent people every day makes it more likely they will ask for help quickly next time.

## If recovery is not working

Recovery can take time, and sometimes the automated process fails. Keep these points in mind:

- Keep trying the official process; submit any requested identity information carefully.
- If you have a business that advertises with Meta, use the official support options available through Meta's business tools.
- Do not create a new account in a way that breaks the platform's rules, and do not pay anyone who claims special access.
- If the compromise involved fraud — for example, money taken from friends or your cards — report it to your bank and to the relevant authority: in the US, the FBI's [Internet Crime Complaint Center](https://www.ic3.gov/) and the FTC; in the UK, [Report Fraud](https://www.reportfraud.police.uk/) (formerly Action Fraud) (or Police Scotland in Scotland).

:::takeaways
- In the first 15 minutes: change your password, log out other sessions, fix contact details, enable two-factor authentication — or start official recovery at facebook.com/hacked.
- Warn friends and secure your email account within the first hour.
- Inspect posts, messages, apps, admins and payment methods within the first day.
- Work out how it happened and fix that weakness during the first week.
- Never pay anyone who offers to recover your account.
:::

:::faq
Q: How long does Facebook account recovery take?
A: If you still have access, you can secure the account in minutes. If you are locked out, the official recovery process can take anywhere from minutes to several days, depending on what identity information Meta asks for and whether your original contact details are still on the account.
Q: Can I recover my Facebook account if the hacker changed my email and phone number?
A: Often, yes. Check your original email for a security notice with an option to reverse the change. If none is available, use facebook.com/hacked and follow the identity confirmation steps, which may include uploading an ID or recording a video selfie in some regions.
Q: Should I delete my Facebook account after it was hacked?
A: Not necessarily. Once you have recovered and secured it with a unique password and two-factor authentication, it can be as safe as any other account. Deleting it in a hurry can also delete content and access you may want later.
Q: Will Facebook notify my friends that I was hacked?
A: Generally, no. You should warn friends yourself so they ignore any messages sent while the attacker had access.
:::
