You do not need to understand how networks work or what encryption algorithms are called to be well protected online. You need a handful of good settings and a few habits. Security agencies such as the US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) and the UK National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) recommend broadly the same short list — and that is what this checklist is built around.

It is organised into four stages. Each stage builds on the last, and the first takes about thirty minutes. If you only ever complete stage one, you will already be safer than a large share of internet users.

:::note How to use this checklist
The checkboxes on this page are interactive so you can track your progress as you go. They are not saved — nothing you tick is stored or sent anywhere — so you may want to keep the page open while you work.
:::

## Stage one: today (about 30 minutes)

These steps protect the accounts that matter most against the attacks that happen most.

:::checklist Today
- [ ] Give your main email account a long, unique passphrase
- [ ] Turn on two-factor authentication for your email account
- [ ] Turn on automatic updates for your phone and computer
- [ ] Set a six-digit or stronger passcode on your phone
- [ ] Decide on one rule: I never share verification codes with anyone
:::

### Why email comes first

Your email account is the master key to your online life. Almost every other service uses it to reset passwords. If someone controls your email, they can take over your other accounts one by one. Protecting it first gives you the largest benefit for a single change. Our guides on [creating strong passwords](/blog/how-to-create-strong-passwords/) and [two-factor authentication](/blog/two-factor-authentication-explained/) show you how.

### Why updates matter so much

Updates fix security weaknesses that criminals already know about. Turning on automatic updates means you are protected without having to remember. It is one of the simplest and most effective things you can do.

## Stage two: this week

Now extend that protection to the rest of your important accounts.

:::checklist This week
- [ ] Start using a password manager (your browser's built-in one is fine to begin with)
- [ ] Give your banking, social media and shopping accounts unique passwords
- [ ] Turn on two-factor authentication for banking, social media and your Apple, Google or Microsoft account
- [ ] Save backup codes for each account somewhere offline
- [ ] Check your email address at Have I Been Pwned and change any exposed passwords
- [ ] Turn on Find My (iPhone) or Find My Device (Android)
:::

A [password manager](/blog/password-managers-explained/) makes unique passwords effortless and has a bonus: it will not fill in your password on a fake website, which helps you spot phishing.

:::tip Don't try to change every password at once
Focus on the important accounts this week. For everything else, update the password the next time you log in and let your password manager remember it. Within a few months, most of your accounts will be covered.
:::

## Stage three: this month

These steps reduce your exposure and prepare you for the day something goes wrong.

:::checklist This month
- [ ] Set up automatic backups for your phone and computer
- [ ] Review app permissions on your phone and delete apps you no longer use
- [ ] Remove browser extensions you do not use
- [ ] Hide your birthday, phone number and address from public social media profiles
- [ ] Set a PIN or password on your mobile carrier account
- [ ] Change your home router's default admin password and update its firmware
- [ ] Delete old online accounts you no longer use
:::

### Backups are your safety net

A recent backup turns disasters — ransomware, a lost phone, a broken laptop — into inconveniences. For extra safety against ransomware, keep at least one backup that is not permanently connected to your computer. [Malware explained](/blog/malware-explained/) covers why.

### Less public information means fewer convincing scams

Scammers use publicly visible details to make their messages believable and to guess security answers. Tidying up your profiles is both a privacy and a security win. See [how to improve your online privacy](/blog/improve-online-privacy/).

## Stage four: everyday habits

Settings protect you automatically. Habits protect you in the moments settings cannot reach.

:::checklist Ongoing habits
- [ ] I do not act on links or phone numbers in unexpected messages — I go to the service directly
- [ ] I check the real web address before entering a password
- [ ] I never approve sign-in prompts I did not start
- [ ] I install software only from official stores and developers
- [ ] I treat urgent, emotional or secretive requests as warning signs
- [ ] I review my accounts' security settings every few months
:::

The most important habit is the first one. Most attacks — phishing emails, scam texts, fake support calls — depend on you acting on something the attacker sent you. Going to the service yourself sidesteps nearly all of them. Our guides on [how phishing attacks work](/blog/how-phishing-attacks-work/) and [social engineering](/blog/social-engineering-explained/) explain the patterns to watch for.

> Good security is not about being suspicious of everything. It is about a few reliable habits that protect you even on a busy day.

## If you look after other people

Many of us help parents, children or less technical friends with their devices. A few extra points:

- **Set up the essentials for them** — updates, a passcode, two-factor authentication on email — and show them where to find recovery codes.
- **Agree a family safe word** that would be used in a genuine emergency call, to defeat impersonation scams.
- **Make it safe to ask.** People who fear looking foolish delay asking for help. A quick "is this real?" message should always be welcome.
- **For children**, combine age-appropriate controls with ongoing conversation about scams, oversharing and who to talk to if something goes wrong.

## How to tell it is working

Security is mostly invisible when it works, which can make it hard to know whether your effort paid off. A few signs that your setup is doing its job:

- **Your password manager stays quiet on a suspicious page.** If it does not offer to autofill, it does not recognise the site — exactly the warning you want.
- **You receive a login alert you can explain.** Alerts for your own new devices show the system is watching.
- **A reset code arrives that you did not request — and nothing else happens.** Someone may have tried your account, but your password and second factor held.
- **Your phone updates itself overnight** without you thinking about it.
- **You hesitate before tapping a link in a text.** That pause is the most valuable habit on this page.

### Keep the momentum

Put a recurring reminder in your calendar every three months titled "security check". When it appears, spend ten minutes reviewing active sessions on your email and main social accounts, removing apps you no longer use, and confirming your backups are recent. Small, regular maintenance is far easier than a big clean-up after something goes wrong.

## If something goes wrong

Even well-protected people are sometimes caught out. If you think an account has been compromised, follow our step-by-step guide on [what to do if your online account has been hacked](/blog/what-to-do-if-account-hacked/). Act quickly, use only official recovery routes, and never pay anyone who offers to "recover" an account for you.

## Your progress at a glance

| Stage | Focus | Time | Protects against |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Today | Email, updates, phone lock | ~30 minutes | Most account takeovers and known software weaknesses |
| This week | Password manager, 2FA everywhere | A few hours spread out | Password reuse, credential stuffing, many phishing attacks |
| This month | Backups, permissions, privacy | An afternoon | Data loss, ransomware, oversharing, SIM swaps |
| Habits | Pause, verify, go direct | Ongoing | Phishing, social engineering, scams |

:::takeaways
- Protect your email first: unique passphrase and two-factor authentication.
- Turn on automatic updates and lock your phone with a strong passcode.
- Use a password manager and two-factor authentication on all important accounts.
- Back up regularly and reduce what you share publicly.
- Build the habit of going directly to services instead of trusting unexpected messages.
:::

:::faq
Q: What is the single most important cybersecurity step for beginners?
A: Securing your main email account with a unique password and two-factor authentication. Because email can reset most other accounts, it provides the biggest protection for one change.
Q: Do I need to pay for security software or services?
A: Not necessarily. Built-in tools — automatic updates, your operating system's security features, browser password managers and free authenticator apps — cover the essentials. Paid tools can add convenience and features, but the habits and settings in this checklist matter most.
Q: How often should I review my security?
A: A quick review every three to six months is a good rhythm: check active sessions on key accounts, remove unused apps and extensions, and confirm your recovery details are current.
Q: Is this checklist enough for a small business?
A: It is an excellent foundation for individuals and very small teams, but businesses have additional needs, such as access management, staff training, incident response and data protection obligations. National agencies such as CISA and the NCSC publish guidance specifically for small organisations.
:::
