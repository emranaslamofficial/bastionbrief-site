Take a breath. Account compromises are extremely common, and most can be fully recovered. What matters now is acting in the right order: first regain control, then remove the intruder's access, then check what else might be affected, and finally close the door they came through.

This guide is deliberately general so that it applies to almost any account. For platform-specific detail, see our guides on [recovering a hacked Facebook account](/blog/recover-hacked-facebook-account/) and [securing your Instagram account](/blog/secure-instagram-account/).

## Start here: what are you seeing?

Different situations call for different first moves. Find the row that best matches yours.

| What you are seeing | What it probably means | Start at |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Login alerts or codes you did not request, but you can still log in | Someone has your password or is trying to | Step 2 |
| Posts, messages or purchases you did not make | Someone has access right now | Step 2 |
| Your password no longer works | The intruder changed it | Step 1 |
| An email saying your email address or phone number was changed | The intruder is locking you out | Step 1 — act immediately |
| Friends receiving strange messages from you | Your account is being used for scams | Step 2, then Step 4 |
| A breach notification from a company | Your password may be exposed, but the account may not be accessed yet | Step 2 |

## Step 1: Regain access

If you are locked out, use **only** the service's official recovery process. Type the address yourself or use the official app — do not follow links from emails or messages you were not expecting, and do not search for "account recovery help" and call the first phone number you find.

:::tip Look for the "this wasn't me" email
When a password, email address or phone number is changed, most major services send a notification to your previous email address or phone. These often include a link to reverse the change and secure the account. Check your inbox and spam folder for recent security messages from the service — and verify the sender carefully.
:::

Official recovery starting points for some of the largest services:

| Service | Official recovery |
| --- | --- |
| Google / Gmail | [accounts.google.com/signin/recovery](https://accounts.google.com/signin/recovery) |
| Microsoft / Outlook | [account.live.com/acsr](https://account.live.com/acsr) |
| Apple Account | [iforgot.apple.com](https://iforgot.apple.com/) |
| Facebook | [facebook.com/hacked](https://www.facebook.com/hacked) |
| Instagram | [instagram.com/hacked](https://www.instagram.com/hacked) |

Recovery may ask for previous passwords, codes sent to old contact details, identity documents or other proof. Answer as accurately as you can. If you are refused at first, try again — sometimes from a device and location you have used before.

:::danger Never pay for account recovery
People who contact you offering to recover a hacked account for a fee — especially in comments, DMs or "recovery expert" adverts — are scammers. Only the service itself can restore your account.
:::

## Step 2: Lock the intruder out

Once you are in, move quickly. The order matters.

1. **Change the password** to a strong one you have never used before. Do this from a device you trust. Our guide to [creating strong passwords](/blog/how-to-create-strong-passwords/) explains what "strong" really means.
2. **Sign out of all other sessions.** Most services have a "log out of all devices" option in their security settings.
3. **Check your recovery details.** Remove any email address, phone number or security question the intruder added, and make sure yours are correct.
4. **Turn on two-factor authentication**, or re-set it up if the intruder may have changed it. Generate new backup codes. See our [two-factor authentication guide](/blog/two-factor-authentication-explained/).
5. **Revoke connected apps and devices** you do not recognise.

## Step 3: Look for what the intruder left behind

Attackers often set things up so they can return or keep collecting information. Check carefully.

:::checklist Hidden changes to check
- [ ] Email forwarding rules — a rule that silently forwards your mail to another address
- [ ] Email filters that delete or archive security alerts
- [ ] Auto-reply messages and changed signatures
- [ ] Linked or "trusted" devices you do not recognise
- [ ] App passwords or API keys created recently
- [ ] Changed profile details, names or photos
- [ ] Saved payment methods, delivery addresses and recent orders
- [ ] Messages sent from your account
:::

Email forwarding rules deserve special emphasis. They are easy to miss and can quietly leak every password-reset email you receive, letting the attacker take over account after account.

## Step 4: Protect the people around you

If your account was used to message others, they may be the next targets.

- **Warn your contacts** through a different channel that your account was compromised and that they should ignore recent requests for money, codes or clicks.
- **Delete scam posts** made in your name.
- **Report impersonation** if the attacker created fake profiles using your identity.

## Step 5: Check your other accounts

One compromise frequently leads to others. Work through this list:

- **Your email account.** If the hacked account was not your email, check the email account linked to it. If your email itself was compromised, assume any account that uses it for password resets could be at risk, and change those passwords too.
- **Accounts that shared the password.** If the hacked password was used anywhere else, change it on every one of those sites now. A [password manager](/blog/password-managers-explained/) prevents this problem in future.
- **Financial accounts.** Check bank, card and payment app activity. Contact your bank using the number on your card if anything looks wrong.

You can also check whether your email address appears in known breaches at [Have I Been Pwned](https://haveibeenpwned.com/).

## Step 6: Work out how it happened

Fixing the entry point prevents a repeat. Think about the days and weeks before the compromise.

:::flow Common entry points
- Phishing: Did you enter your password on a page you reached from a link?
- Shared code: Did anyone ask you to forward a verification code?
- Password reuse: Was the password used on another site that may have been breached?
- Malware: Did you install anything unusual, or download a file from a stranger?
- Device access: Could someone have used your phone or computer directly?
:::

If phishing is likely, read [how to identify a fake login page](/blog/how-to-identify-fake-login-page/). If malware is possible — for example, if accounts keep being compromised even after password changes — follow the steps in [malware explained](/blog/malware-explained/) before changing passwords again, ideally from a different device.

## Step 7: Report it

Reporting helps platforms and authorities act against attackers, and creates a record if you need one later.

- **Report to the service** through its official help or security channels.
- **Report fraud** if money was lost or your identity was misused. In the US, use the FTC's [IdentityTheft.gov](https://www.identitytheft.gov/) and the FBI's [IC3](https://www.ic3.gov/). In the UK, report to [Report Fraud](https://www.reportfraud.police.uk/) (formerly Action Fraud) (or Police Scotland in Scotland).
- **Tell your employer** immediately if a work account or work device was involved.

## Step 8: Harden for the future

Once things are stable, spend a little time making a repeat much less likely:

:::checklist After-incident hardening
- [ ] Every important account has a unique password stored in a password manager
- [ ] Email, banking and social media all use two-factor authentication
- [ ] Backup codes are saved somewhere offline
- [ ] Login alerts are switched on where available
- [ ] Devices and browsers are fully updated
- [ ] I have removed apps and extensions I do not use
:::

Our [beginner's cybersecurity checklist](/blog/cybersecurity-checklist-for-beginners/) is a good next step.

> The goal is not to never be targeted. It is to make sure that next time, the attempt bounces off.

:::takeaways
- Use only official recovery routes; never pay "recovery experts".
- After regaining access: change the password, sign out everywhere, fix recovery details, enable 2FA.
- Check for forwarding rules, unknown devices and other changes the intruder left behind.
- Warn contacts, check other accounts, and change any reused passwords.
- Work out how it happened and fix that weakness.
:::

:::faq
Q: How do I know if my account has been hacked?
A: Common signs include login alerts you did not trigger, password-reset emails you did not request, posts or messages you did not send, changed settings or recovery details, and being unexpectedly logged out with a password that no longer works.
Q: Should I delete a hacked account?
A: Usually not straight away. Recovering and securing the account lets you remove what the attacker did, warn contacts and preserve your data. You can still delete it later if you no longer want it.
Q: Can a hacker get back in after I change my password?
A: Yes, if other routes remain open — for example, an active session that was not signed out, a recovery email they added, a forwarding rule, a connected app, or malware on your device. That is why steps 2 and 3 of this guide matter.
Q: Will changing my password log the hacker out?
A: On many services it does, but not always. Explicitly using the "sign out of all devices" option is the most reliable way to end other sessions.
Q: Someone is threatening to leak my information unless I pay. What should I do?
A: Do not pay and do not engage further. Save evidence, secure your accounts using the steps above, and report the threat to the platform and to the police or relevant authority.
:::
