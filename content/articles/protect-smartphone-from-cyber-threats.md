A modern smartphone is arguably the most sensitive device you own. It unlocks your email, approves your bank payments, receives your two-factor codes, stores years of photos and messages, and knows where you are. At the same time, it lives in pockets, bags, taxis and café tables.

The good news is that iOS and Android are built with strong security foundations: apps are isolated from one another, app stores review software, and storage is encrypted when the device is locked. Most real-world phone compromises come not from breaking those protections but from going around them — through theft, deception, or someone with access to your phone.

## The threat map

| Threat | How it usually happens | Main defence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Theft or loss | Phone is stolen, sometimes after the thief has watched you type your passcode | Strong passcode, biometrics, theft-protection features, remote wipe |
| Smishing | Texts with links to fake delivery, bank or toll pages | Do not tap links in unexpected texts; use official apps |
| Malicious apps | Apps installed from outside official stores, or apps requesting excessive permissions | Official stores only, permission reviews |
| SIM swap | A criminal persuades your carrier to move your number to their SIM | Carrier account PIN, app-based two-factor authentication |
| Stalkerware | Someone with access installs monitoring software | Strong passcode, checks for unknown apps and profiles |
| Outdated software | Known vulnerabilities in old system versions | Automatic updates; replace unsupported phones |
| Account compromise | Your Apple or Google account is taken over | Unique password, two-factor authentication, recovery options |

## Lock it down: passcode and biometrics

Everything else depends on the lock screen. When a phone is locked, its storage is encrypted and its data is protected — but only as well as the passcode.

- Use **at least a six-digit passcode**, or better, an alphanumeric one. Avoid birthdays, repeated digits and simple patterns.
- Enable **Face ID, Touch ID or fingerprint unlock** for convenience, so a stronger passcode is not a burden.
- Set the phone to **lock automatically** after a short period.
- Hide sensitive content in **lock-screen notifications**, so codes and messages are not readable without unlocking.

:::warning Guard your passcode in public
Some phone thefts involve watching a victim enter their passcode before stealing the phone. With the passcode, a thief may be able to change account settings and lock the owner out. Use biometrics in public where possible, and shield the screen when you must type your passcode.
:::

### Turn on theft protection

Both major platforms now include features designed specifically for stolen phones:

- **iPhone:** **Stolen Device Protection** requires Face ID or Touch ID — with no passcode fallback — for sensitive actions such as changing your Apple Account password when you are away from familiar locations, and can add a security delay.
- **Android:** recent versions include **theft protection** features, such as locking the screen automatically if the phone appears to be snatched, and requiring extra verification for sensitive settings. Availability depends on your Android version and manufacturer.

Make sure **Find My** (Apple) or **Find My Device** (Google) is enabled so you can locate, lock or erase the phone remotely.

## Keep it updated

Phone updates regularly fix security vulnerabilities, some of which are actively exploited. Turn on automatic updates for the operating system and apps.

Phones eventually stop receiving updates. Check how long your manufacturer supports your model; once security updates stop, the phone becomes progressively riskier for banking, email and other sensitive tasks.

## Be selective about apps

The official app stores are not perfect, but they are far safer than installing apps from websites, messages or file-sharing services.

- **Install apps only from the App Store or Google Play** (or your manufacturer's official store). On Android, avoid enabling installation from unknown sources.
- **Check before installing:** the developer's name, the number of downloads, recent reviews, and whether the permissions make sense for what the app does.
- **Review permissions regularly.** A torch app has no need for your contacts. A game does not need your microphone. Both iOS and Android let you review and revoke permissions in Settings.
- **Delete apps you no longer use.** Fewer apps means fewer potential weaknesses.

:::tip Use the built-in privacy dashboards
iOS's **App Privacy Report** and Android's **Privacy dashboard** show which apps have accessed your location, camera, microphone and other sensitive data recently. They are an easy way to spot an app behaving unexpectedly.
:::

## Handle messages with care

Smishing — phishing by text — is one of the most common phone threats. Messages about missed parcels, unpaid tolls, bank alerts, tax refunds and prize claims are all familiar lures. Our guide to [how phishing attacks work](/blog/how-phishing-attacks-work/) explains the patterns in detail.

The rule for phones is simple: **do not tap links in unexpected messages.** If the message claims to be from a company you deal with, open its official app or type its address yourself. In the US and UK, you can forward scam texts to **7726** to report them to your mobile network.

## Protect your phone number

Your phone number is used for account recovery and, often, text-message codes. That makes it a target for **SIM swapping**, where a criminal convinces your mobile carrier to transfer your number to a SIM card they control.

- **Set a PIN or password on your mobile carrier account**, and ask whether your carrier offers extra protection against SIM changes or number transfers ("port-out" protection).
- **Move important accounts from SMS codes to an authenticator app or passkey.** See our [two-factor authentication guide](/blog/two-factor-authentication-explained/).
- **Be alert to sudden loss of signal.** If your phone unexpectedly shows "No service" in an area where it normally works, contact your carrier from another phone quickly.

## Stalkerware and unwanted monitoring

Stalkerware is monitoring software installed on a phone without the owner's meaningful consent, often by a partner, ex-partner or family member with physical access. It can expose messages, location, photos and calls.

Possible signs include unfamiliar apps (sometimes disguised with generic names), unexplained battery drain or data usage, settings changed without your knowledge, and — most tellingly — someone knowing things they should not.

:::danger If you suspect someone is monitoring you
If you are in a situation involving domestic abuse, removing monitoring software can alert the person who installed it and may escalate risk. Consider reaching out to a specialist support organisation for safety planning before making changes. The Coalition Against Stalkerware lists resources for affected people.
:::

On iPhone, also check **Settings → General → VPN & Device Management** for configuration profiles you did not install. On Android, review apps with **device admin** or **accessibility** permissions, which monitoring apps often request.

## Secure the accounts behind the phone

Your phone is tied to an Apple Account or Google Account that can locate, back up and sometimes erase the device. If that account is compromised, the attacker gains a great deal.

:::checklist Account essentials
- [ ] My Apple or Google account has a unique, strong password
- [ ] Two-factor authentication is on for that account
- [ ] Recovery email and phone number are current
- [ ] I have reviewed which devices are signed in to my account and removed old ones
:::

## Back up regularly

Backups protect you from theft, loss, damage and ransomware alike. Enable iCloud Backup or Google's backup service, or back up regularly to a computer. Make sure photos and messages — usually the things people miss most — are included.

## For people at higher risk

Journalists, activists, public figures and people facing targeted threats can enable extra protections. Apple's **Lockdown Mode** severely restricts features commonly abused by highly targeted spyware. Google offers **Advanced Protection** for Google accounts, and newer Android versions include an Advanced Protection mode for devices. These features trade convenience for security and are not necessary for most people.

## Your smartphone security checklist

:::checklist Do these today
- [ ] Six-digit or stronger passcode, with biometrics enabled
- [ ] Automatic updates turned on
- [ ] Find My / Find My Device enabled
- [ ] Stolen Device Protection (iPhone) or theft protection (Android) enabled
- [ ] Lock-screen notifications set to hide sensitive content
- [ ] Unused apps deleted and permissions reviewed
- [ ] Carrier account PIN set
- [ ] Backups enabled
:::

For connecting your phone safely away from home, read [how public Wi-Fi can put your data at risk](/blog/public-wifi-risks/). To understand malicious apps and other threats more broadly, see [malware explained](/blog/malware-explained/).

:::takeaways
- Most phone compromises come from theft, deception or physical access, not from breaking the operating system.
- A strong passcode, biometrics and theft-protection features are the foundation.
- Install apps only from official stores and review permissions regularly.
- Protect your number from SIM swaps with a carrier PIN and app-based two-factor authentication.
- Keep the phone updated and backed up.
:::

:::faq
Q: Do I need antivirus software on my phone?
A: For most iPhone users, no — the system's design limits what third-party antivirus apps can do. On Android, Google Play Protect is built in and scans apps. Avoid "cleaner" and "booster" apps with alarming claims. Keeping your phone updated and installing apps only from official stores matters far more.
Q: Is iPhone more secure than Android?
A: Both platforms have strong security when kept updated. Differences in how quickly updates arrive and how apps are distributed matter more than the brand. A recent Android phone that receives regular updates and an iPhone are both reasonable choices.
Q: What should I do immediately if my phone is stolen?
A: Use Find My or Find My Device from another device to lock or erase it, contact your carrier to suspend the SIM, change the password of your Apple or Google account, and watch for unexpected account changes. Report the theft to the police if appropriate.
Q: Can someone hack my phone by calling me?
A: An ordinary phone call cannot install malware on an updated phone. However, callers commonly use social engineering to get you to share codes, install apps or reveal information. Hang up on suspicious calls and call back using a known number.
:::
