Long before computers, confidence tricksters made a living by persuading people to hand over money, keys or secrets. The tools have changed — emails, text messages, phone calls, social media and now synthetic voices — but the method is the same. Social engineering is the manipulation of people into breaking normal security practices, usually by making them believe they are doing something reasonable, helpful or urgent.

It matters because it sidesteps technology. A company can have excellent firewalls and still lose millions because an employee was persuaded to change a supplier's bank details. You can have a strong password and still lose your account because you read a code aloud to a convincing caller. Understanding social engineering is not about becoming suspicious of everyone. It is about recognising a handful of patterns so that you can pause at the right moment.

## The levers: what attackers actually exploit

Most social engineering pulls on a small number of psychological levers. None of them are character flaws. They are the same instincts that make cooperation and community possible. Attackers simply borrow them.

### Authority

We tend to comply with people who appear to have the right to ask. A caller claiming to be from your bank's fraud team, an email from "the CEO", a message from "IT support" or a letter on official-looking paper all borrow authority.

**What it sounds like:** *"This is the fraud department. We've frozen a suspicious transaction and need to confirm your identity."*

**The counter:** Authority is easy to claim and hard to prove in a single message. Hang up and call the organisation back on a number you find yourself.

### Urgency

Time pressure stops us from checking. Deadlines, countdowns and "act now" warnings are designed to push you past the moment where you would normally ask a question.

**What it sounds like:** *"Your account will be permanently deleted in 24 hours unless you verify."*

**The counter:** Genuine organisations rarely require irreversible action within minutes. Urgency itself is a reason to slow down.

### Fear

Threats of account closure, legal action, arrest, unpaid tax or exposure trigger a stress response that narrows our thinking. Some scams combine fear with secrecy: *"Don't tell anyone, or it will make things worse."*

**The counter:** Real authorities do not demand payment in gift cards or cryptocurrency, and they do not forbid you from speaking to family or a lawyer.

### Helpfulness and reciprocity

Most people like to help, especially colleagues and friends. Attackers ask for small favours — forward a code, open a file, hold a door — and sometimes offer a small favour first so you feel obliged to return it.

**What it sounds like:** *"Hey, I accidentally sent my login code to your number, could you send it back?"*

**The counter:** A favour that involves a code, a password, a payment or access is never a small favour. Verify through another channel.

### Familiarity and liking

We trust people and brands we know. Compromised accounts belonging to friends, lookalike profiles of colleagues, and messages that reference shared interests or recent events all exploit this.

**The counter:** A familiar name on a message proves only that someone has access to that name. If the request is unusual, check with the person directly.

### Scarcity and opportunity

Limited-time offers, exclusive invitations, unexpected refunds and prizes appeal to our fear of missing out.

**The counter:** If you did not enter, you did not win. If an offer requires you to pay or log in first, walk away.

> Social engineers do not need you to be gullible. They need you to be busy, kind, worried or hopeful — which describes everyone some of the time.

## The techniques

The levers above are combined into recognisable techniques. You will see most of them across email, text, phone and in person.

| Technique | How it works | Typical example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Phishing | Deceptive messages that lead to fake pages, malicious files or harmful replies | An email about a "suspended account" with a login link |
| Pretexting | Inventing a believable scenario (the pretext) to justify a request | A caller posing as an auditor who needs "just a few details" |
| Vishing | Social engineering by voice call | A fake bank fraud team asking you to read out a code |
| Baiting | Offering something tempting to get you to take a risky action | A free download, or a USB stick left in a car park |
| Quid pro quo | Offering a service in exchange for access | Fake tech support offering to "fix" your computer remotely |
| Tailgating | Following an authorised person through a secure door | Someone carrying boxes asking you to hold the door |
| Impersonation | Pretending to be a specific trusted person | A message from a "manager" asking for an urgent payment |

### Pretexting: the story is the attack

Pretexting is worth singling out, because it underpins many of the others. The attacker builds a scenario in which their request makes sense. A "new contractor" who needs Wi-Fi access. A "delivery driver" who needs your one-time code to confirm the drop-off. An "HR officer" verifying payroll details.

Good pretexts are researched. Information from company websites, professional networking profiles and social media can make a story very convincing. The more an unexpected caller seems to know, the more natural it feels to fill in the gaps — which is exactly what they want.

### Synthetic voices and video

Tools that imitate a person's voice from short audio samples are now widely available. Law enforcement agencies, including the FBI, have warned that criminals use AI-generated content to make impersonation scams more convincing — for example, a call that sounds like a family member in distress, or a video meeting featuring a fake executive.

You do not need to become an expert in detecting synthetic media. The same defence works: verify unusual requests through a channel you control.

:::tip Set up a family safe word
Agree on a word or phrase with close family members that you would use in a genuine emergency. If you receive a distressing call asking for money, ask for the word. It costs nothing and defeats voice-cloning scams entirely.
:::

## The three-question pause

You cannot memorise every scam. Instead, build a habit that triggers whenever a request involves money, access, codes, passwords or personal data. Ask yourself three questions:

:::flow The three-question pause
- Did I expect this?: Unexpected requests deserve extra care, especially if they arrive out of nowhere.
- Is someone rushing me?: Urgency, fear or secrecy is a warning sign in itself.
- Can I verify it my way?: Use a phone number, app or website you find yourself — never the one supplied.
:::

If the answers are "no", "yes" and "not yet", stop. Verification usually takes a minute. Most attacks collapse the moment you step outside the channel the attacker controls.

## Social engineering at work

Organisations are frequent targets because a single successful deception can unlock money or data at scale. A few habits make a large difference:

- **Verify payment and bank-detail changes** by calling a known number, never one in the request. This alone prevents a great deal of invoice fraud.
- **Never share login codes or approve sign-in prompts you did not start**, even for "IT". Real IT teams do not need your codes.
- **Challenge politely.** "Can I call you back on the main switchboard?" is a perfectly professional response.
- **Report attempts**, even ones you spotted. Your security team can warn colleagues who may receive the same message.
- **Make it safe to report mistakes.** People who fear blame hide incidents; people who feel supported report them quickly, when it matters most.

## Social engineering at home

At home, the most common forms are phishing messages, fake delivery texts, tech-support scams, romance and investment scams, and account-takeover attempts through compromised friends' accounts.

:::checklist Protect yourself and your family
- [ ] Never share verification codes with anyone, including people claiming to be from the company
- [ ] Hang up on unexpected calls about money or security and call back on a known number
- [ ] Never let a stranger remotely access your computer
- [ ] Agree a family safe word for emergencies
- [ ] Limit how much personal information is visible on your public social profiles
- [ ] Talk with older relatives and teenagers about current scams — both groups are frequently targeted
:::

If you have shared information or access with someone you now suspect, act quickly. ## Why technology still matters

Social engineering targets people, but technology can blunt its impact. [Password managers](/blog/password-managers-explained/) refuse to fill credentials on fake sites. Passkeys and hardware security keys cannot be phished, because they only work with the genuine website. And [two-factor authentication](/blog/two-factor-authentication-explained/) means a stolen password alone is no longer enough. The best defence combines a thoughtful pause with tools that make the wrong action harder to take.

:::faq
Q: What is the most common form of social engineering?
A: Phishing — deceptive messages by email, text or social media — is the most widespread form. Phone-based scams (vishing) and impersonation of colleagues or friends are also very common.
Q: How can I tell if a phone call from my bank is genuine?
A: You often cannot tell from the call itself, because caller ID can be spoofed. Hang up, wait a moment, and call the number printed on your card or the bank's official website. A genuine bank will understand.
Q: Are only older people targeted by social engineering?
A: No. Different scams target different groups. Older adults are often targeted by tech-support and impersonation scams, while younger people are frequently targeted through social media, gaming platforms, job offers and online shopping.
Q: Is it rude to verify someone's identity before helping them?
A: Not at all. Verification is standard practice in any security-conscious organisation, and genuine colleagues and companies expect it. A legitimate person will not be offended by a call-back.
:::
