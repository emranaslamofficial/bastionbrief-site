Phishing is a message pretending to be from someone you trust, designed to get you to do something that benefits the sender: type a password into a fake page, open a malicious attachment, approve a payment or hand over personal details. The name is a deliberate misspelling of "fishing" — the attacker casts out bait and waits for a bite.

It has been around for decades, and it remains one of the most common starting points for account takeovers and data breaches. That is not because people are foolish. It is because phishing is designed by people who study how attention, trust and urgency work, and who only need to succeed occasionally to make it worthwhile.

## Anatomy of a phishing email

Let us take apart a typical example. Imagine this message arrives in your inbox:

:::note An example phishing email (fictional)
**From:** Account Security <no-reply@secure-accounts-verify.example>

**Subject:** Unusual sign-in attempt — action required

We detected an unusual sign-in attempt on your account from a new device. For your protection, your account has been temporarily limited.

To restore full access, verify your identity within 24 hours. Accounts not verified will be permanently suspended.

[Verify my account]
:::

Each part of this message has been engineered:

- **The sender name** says "Account Security", which is what your eye reads first. The actual address belongs to a domain that has nothing to do with any real company.
- **The subject line** borrows the language of genuine security alerts. Real services do send messages like this, which is why fake ones are effective.
- **The problem** is plausible and alarming. A new device trying to sign in is exactly the kind of thing you would want to know about.
- **The deadline** — 24 hours — exists to stop you from thinking it over or checking another way.
- **The threat** of permanent suspension raises the stakes.
- **The button** hides the real destination behind friendly text.

Notice that nothing here is technically sophisticated. The message is effective because it is emotionally well designed.

## The five ingredients of almost every phish

Strip away the branding and the specifics, and most phishing messages combine some or all of the same ingredients:

:::flow The phishing recipe
- Impersonation: Pretends to be a trusted brand, colleague, friend or authority.
- A reason: Gives you a problem or opportunity — a locked account, a parcel, a refund.
- Urgency: Adds a deadline or threat so you act before thinking.
- An action: Asks you to click, log in, download, reply or pay.
- A capture point: A fake page, attachment or reply that collects what the attacker wants.
:::

Once you start looking for these ingredients rather than for specific scams, you will recognise new variations you have never seen before.

## Types of phishing

Phishing is not limited to email. The same recipe is served through many channels.

| Type | Channel | What it typically looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bulk phishing | Email | Generic messages sent to huge numbers of people, impersonating banks, streaming services or delivery companies |
| Spear phishing | Email or social media | Personalised messages that use your name, role, employer or recent activity to seem credible |
| Whaling | Email | Spear phishing aimed at senior executives, often about legal, financial or confidential matters |
| Business email compromise | Email | A message appearing to come from a boss, colleague or supplier asking for a payment or bank detail change |
| Smishing | SMS and messaging apps | Texts about missed deliveries, unpaid tolls, bank alerts or prize claims with a short link |
| Vishing | Phone calls | A caller posing as your bank, a tech-support desk or a government agency |
| Quishing | QR codes | A QR code — on a poster, parking meter, email or letter — leading to a fake payment or login page |
| Clone phishing | Email | A copy of a real message you received earlier, with the link or attachment swapped for a malicious one |
| Social media phishing | DMs and comments | Fake support accounts, prize giveaways, "is this you in this video?" links and brand-deal offers |

Some of these deserve a closer look.

### Spear phishing feels personal because it is

Attackers can learn a great deal from public sources: your job title from a professional networking profile, your colleagues' names, the conference you just attended, the supplier your company uses. A message that references real details feels far more trustworthy than a generic one. The defence is to remember that knowing your details does not prove someone is who they say they are.

### Business email compromise often has no link at all

Some of the most costly phishing contains no malicious link or attachment, so security filters may not flag it. It is simply a well-timed email: *"Our bank details have changed, please use the account below for this month's invoice."* The fix is a process, not a tool: verify any change to payment details by calling a known phone number — not one included in the email.

### Smishing exploits small screens

On a phone, you see less of the address, links are often shortened, and messages arrive alongside genuine texts from friends. Texts about parcels, toll charges and bank alerts are common lures. If you receive one, go to the company's app or type its address yourself rather than tapping the link.

### Quishing hides the destination

A QR code is just a link you cannot read. Criminals have placed stickers over legitimate QR codes on parking meters and posters, and QR codes in emails can slip past filters that scan text links. Before entering anything on a page you reached by QR code, check the address in your browser carefully.

## Why phishing works on smart people

It is easy to assume that only careless people fall for phishing. The evidence from security awareness work suggests otherwise. Phishing exploits ordinary features of human attention:

- **We are busy.** A message read between meetings gets less scrutiny than one read with a cup of tea.
- **We respond to authority.** Messages from "IT", "the bank" or "the CEO" carry built-in credibility.
- **We fear loss more than we value gain.** "Your account will be closed" is more motivating than "you could win".
- **We are helpful.** Many scams simply ask for a favour.
- **We pattern-match.** If something looks like the hundred genuine alerts you have received, your brain files it as genuine.

Our deeper guide to [social engineering](/blog/social-engineering-explained/) explores these psychological levers in detail.

> You do not beat phishing by being smarter than the attacker. You beat it by having a habit that does not depend on how convincing the message is.

## How to recognise a phishing message

No single sign is definitive, but the more of these you notice, the more cautious you should be.

### Check who it is really from

Look at the actual sender address, not just the display name. On mobile, tap the sender name to reveal it. Be alert to domains that look close to a real one but are not quite right — extra words, swapped letters, or an unfamiliar ending.

### Check where links really go

On a computer, hover over a link to see its destination before clicking. On a phone, press and hold. Read the domain carefully: the part that matters is the registered domain just before the first single slash. We explain this in depth in [how to identify a fake login page](/blog/how-to-identify-fake-login-page/).

### Notice the emotional pressure

Deadlines, threats, unexpected prizes and appeals to secrecy ("don't tell anyone about this yet") are all warning signs. Legitimate organisations rarely need you to act within minutes.

### Be suspicious of unexpected requests

An unexpected attachment, a request for a code, a sudden change of payment details or an instruction to buy gift cards should all trigger a pause, no matter who appears to be asking.

:::tip The habit that beats almost every phish
Never act on the link or phone number in an unexpected message. Instead, go to the service yourself — open the official app, type the address you know, or call a number from the back of your card or a previous bill. If the problem is real, you will see it there.
:::

## Technology that helps

Good habits are the foundation, but some tools make phishing much less effective:

- **Password managers** will not autofill your password on a site whose address does not match the one saved. If autofill unexpectedly does nothing, stop and check where you are. See [password managers explained](/blog/password-managers-explained/).
- **Passkeys and security keys** are designed so that they only work on the genuine site. Even a perfect fake page cannot capture them. Our [two-factor authentication guide](/blog/two-factor-authentication-explained/) explains why.
- **Email filtering and browser warnings** catch many known phishing pages. Do not click through warnings that say a site is deceptive.

## What to do if you clicked

Clicking a link is not automatically a disaster. What matters is what you did next.

1. **If you only opened the page** and entered nothing, close it. Run your device's security software if you are concerned.
2. **If you entered a password**, change it immediately on the real site — and anywhere else you used it. Turn on two-factor authentication.
3. **If you entered a code or approved a sign-in prompt**, change your password and review active sessions, signing out any you do not recognise.
4. **If you entered card or bank details**, contact your bank straight away using the number on your card.
5. **If you downloaded and opened a file**, disconnect from the network and follow the steps in our [malware guide](/blog/malware-explained/).
6. **If it was a work account**, tell your IT or security team immediately. Speed matters far more than embarrassment.

If an account has already been taken over, follow our step-by-step guide on [what to do if your online account has been hacked](/blog/what-to-do-if-account-hacked/).

## Reporting phishing

Reporting helps others. Most email services have a "report phishing" option. In addition:

- In the US, you can report phishing to the Federal Trade Commission at [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/) and forward phishing emails to the Anti-Phishing Working Group at reportphishing@apwg.org.
- In the UK, you can forward suspicious emails to the National Cyber Security Centre's Suspicious Email Reporting Service at report@phishing.gov.uk.
- In both countries, suspicious text messages can be forwarded to **7726** on most mobile networks.

:::takeaways
- Phishing works by combining impersonation, a believable reason, urgency and a request for action.
- It arrives through email, texts, calls, social media and QR codes — the recipe is the same.
- Check the real sender and the real link destination, and notice emotional pressure.
- The most reliable defence is to never act on links or numbers in unexpected messages; go to the service directly.
- Password managers and passkeys make phishing far less effective.
:::

:::faq
Q: Can you get hacked just by opening a phishing email?
A: Simply opening an email is very unlikely to compromise a modern, updated device. The risk comes from clicking links, opening attachments, entering information or replying. Keep your email app and operating system updated to minimise the remaining risk.
Q: Why do phishing emails sometimes have perfect grammar now?
A: Spelling mistakes were once a reliable clue, but attackers increasingly use templates copied from real messages and writing tools that produce fluent text. Do not rely on bad grammar as a signal; focus on the sender, the link and the request instead.
Q: Is it safe to click "unsubscribe" in a suspicious email?
A: It is safer not to. In a phishing email, the unsubscribe link may lead to a malicious page or simply confirm that your address is active. Mark the message as spam or phishing instead.
Q: What is the difference between phishing and spam?
A: Spam is unwanted bulk messaging, usually advertising. Phishing is deliberately deceptive and aims to steal information, money or access. Some spam is also phishing, but not all spam is malicious.
:::
