Privacy is often framed as an all-or-nothing choice: either you accept that everything about you is known, or you abandon modern life. Neither is true. Privacy is a spectrum, and a handful of thoughtful changes can significantly reduce how much of your life is collected, combined and sold.

It also overlaps with security. Information you share publicly — your birthday, your pet's name, your employer, your holiday dates — is exactly what scammers use to make phishing convincing and to answer security questions. Reducing your data footprint makes you a harder target as well as a more private person.

## Start with your own threat model

Before changing settings, it helps to ask a few questions. Security professionals call this **threat modelling**, and the Electronic Frontier Foundation's Surveillance Self-Defense guide frames it well:

- **What do I want to protect?** Your location, your relationships, your finances, your health, your browsing, your identity?
- **Who do I want to protect it from?** Advertisers, data brokers, scammers, an ex-partner, an employer, the general public?
- **How bad would it be if they got it?**
- **How much effort am I willing to spend?**

Your answers shape your priorities. Someone escaping an abusive relationship needs very different protections from someone who mainly dislikes targeted ads. Most people sit somewhere in between: they want to limit tracking, avoid scams and keep personal details out of strangers' hands.

## Who collects your data

| Collector | What they typically gather | How you reduce it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Social media platforms | Posts, contacts, interests, location, activity on other sites | Privacy settings, ad settings, sharing less |
| Advertisers and trackers | Browsing behaviour across websites and apps | Tracking protection, app tracking settings |
| Data brokers | Names, addresses, phone numbers, relatives, purchase and property records | Opt-out requests, deletion rights |
| Apps | Location, contacts, device identifiers | Permission reviews, deleting unused apps |
| Search engines and browsers | Search history, sites visited | Privacy-focused settings or alternatives |
| Strangers and scammers | Whatever is publicly visible | Profile audits, limiting public information |

## Level one: an afternoon

These steps take a few hours at most and deliver the biggest improvement for the effort.

### Audit your public social profiles

Look at your profiles while logged out, or using the "view as public" option where available. Ask: what could a stranger learn here?

- Remove or hide your **date of birth, phone number, home town and email address** from public view.
- Consider making accounts **private** if you do not need a public audience.
- Review **old posts and photos** — especially ones showing your home, your children's school, your car registration or travel plans.
- Limit who can **tag** you or see your **friends and followers lists**.

### Turn down ad personalisation

Google, Meta, Apple, Microsoft and others offer settings to limit personalised advertising and the use of your activity on other websites. They do not stop collection entirely, but they reduce how your data is used. Look for "Ad settings", "Ad preferences" or "Privacy" in each account.

### Review app permissions

On your phone, go through which apps can access your **location, contacts, photos, microphone and camera**. Switch location to "While using the app" or "Never" unless an app genuinely needs it. On iPhone, consider disabling **Allow Apps to Request to Track**. Delete apps you no longer use. Our [smartphone security guide](/blog/protect-smartphone-from-cyber-threats/) covers this in more detail.

### Choose a browser that blocks trackers

Modern browsers such as Firefox, Safari and Brave include tracking protection that blocks many cross-site trackers by default. Whichever browser you use, review its privacy settings and consider blocking third-party cookies.

:::tip Privacy and security pay off together
Every detail you remove from public view is one less detail a scammer can use. Security questions, phishing messages and impersonation attempts all draw on publicly available information — a theme explored in [social engineering explained](/blog/social-engineering-explained/).
:::

## Level two: a weekend

### Separate your email identities

Using one email address for everything links all your accounts together and makes your address a single target for phishing and spam. Consider:

- a **private address** for banking, government and close contacts;
- a **general address** for shopping and newsletters;
- **email aliases** — forwarding addresses that hide your real one. Several email and password-manager services offer alias features, letting you create a unique address for each site and disable it if it starts receiving spam.

### Tackle data brokers

Data brokers compile profiles from public records, purchases and online activity, and sell them. People-search sites — which can reveal your address, relatives and phone number to anyone — are the most visible part of this industry.

You can request removal from many of these sites through their opt-out pages. It is tedious but effective, and many people start with the largest sites in their country. Paid services will submit requests on your behalf. Depending on where you live, you may have legal rights to demand deletion: in the European Union and UK under data protection law, and in some US states, such as California, under state privacy laws.

### Use your data rights

If you live in the EU or UK, the GDPR (and UK GDPR) gives you the right to access the personal data organisations hold about you, to correct it, and in many cases to have it erased. Several US states provide similar rights. A short, polite request citing the relevant law is usually enough to begin.

### Clean up old accounts

Every forgotten account is a potential breach waiting to happen. Search your email for "welcome" and "verify your account" messages to find services you have signed up for, then delete the ones you no longer use. A [password manager](/blog/password-managers-explained/) can help you keep track of what remains.

## Level three: ongoing habits

### Use end-to-end encrypted messaging

End-to-end encryption means only you and the people you are talking to can read your messages — not the service provider. Signal is widely recommended by privacy advocates for its strong encryption and minimal data collection. WhatsApp and iMessage also use end-to-end encryption for message content, though they collect more metadata.

### Think before you share

A simple pause before posting goes a long way. Avoid sharing travel plans before or during trips, real-time locations, photos that reveal your address, and details commonly used for security questions.

### Be cautious with "free" tools

Free apps, quizzes, VPNs and browser extensions often pay for themselves with your data. Read what permissions they request and who runs them.

### Review settings periodically

Platforms change their privacy settings and defaults over time. A quick review every few months — perhaps alongside a [security check](/blog/cybersecurity-checklist-for-beginners/) — keeps you on top of changes.

> Privacy is not something you achieve once. It is a set of small decisions that add up.

## A note on VPNs and privacy

VPNs are heavily marketed as privacy tools. They do hide your browsing from your internet provider and the local network, but they do not stop websites, advertisers or platforms from tracking you when you are logged in, and they shift your trust to the VPN company. They can be useful, especially on [public Wi-Fi](/blog/public-wifi-risks/), but they are one tool among many rather than a complete privacy solution.

:::checklist Your privacy plan
- [ ] I have checked my social profiles as a stranger would see them
- [ ] Birthday, phone number and address are hidden from public view
- [ ] Ad personalisation is limited on my main accounts
- [ ] App permissions are reviewed and location access is restricted
- [ ] My browser blocks cross-site trackers
- [ ] I use separate email addresses or aliases for different purposes
- [ ] I have requested removal from major people-search sites
- [ ] I have deleted accounts I no longer use
:::

:::takeaways
- Start by deciding what you want to protect and from whom.
- An afternoon of profile, ad and permission changes makes a big difference.
- Email aliases, data broker opt-outs and deleting old accounts go further.
- Privacy and security reinforce each other: less public data means fewer convincing scams.
- Revisit your settings every few months.
:::

:::faq
Q: Is it possible to be completely private online?
A: Not realistically, if you use mainstream services. But you can dramatically reduce what is collected and who can see it, which is what matters for most people.
Q: Does incognito or private browsing mode protect my privacy?
A: Private browsing stops your browser from saving history and cookies after you close the window. It does not hide your activity from websites, your employer, your internet provider or anyone monitoring the network.
Q: How do I find out what data a company holds about me?
A: In the EU and UK, you can make a subject access request under data protection law. Many large companies also offer a "download your data" tool in their privacy settings. Some US states provide similar access rights.
Q: Are people-search sites legal?
A: In many places, yes, because they compile information from public records and other sources. However, many offer opt-out processes, and some jurisdictions give residents a legal right to have their information deleted.
:::
